# Script Templates

Three episode structures for Radical Concepts. The n8n script-generation node selects a template based on episode type, then fills each section from scored and selected articles.

---

## Template A: Standard Daily (5 minutes)

The default weekday format. Two stories from different domains, connected by Brett's analysis.

```
[HOOK - 20 seconds]
Open with a provocative question or observation that connects today's two stories.
Do NOT summarise the stories here. Tease the tension or surprise.
Example: "What do a semiconductor factory in Arizona and a fungus in the Amazon
have in common? More than you'd think."

[STORY 1 - 2 minutes]
Full narrative arc for the lead story.
- Set the scene: who, what, where, when.
- The key fact or development that makes this newsworthy today.
- One or two supporting data points or quotes (attributed to source).
- Why it matters beyond the headline.

[BRETT'S TAKE - 45 seconds]
Brett's personal analysis of Story 1.
- Second-order implications.
- What most coverage is missing.
- A specific thing to watch for next.

[STORY 2 - 2 minutes]
Full narrative arc for the second story. Different domain from Story 1.
- Same structure as Story 1: scene, key fact, data, significance.
- Should feel like a deliberate contrast or complement to Story 1.

[BRETT'S TAKE - 45 seconds]
Analysis of Story 2, then a cross-domain connection back to Story 1.
- Draw the thread between the two stories.
- Identify the shared pattern, tension, or implication.

[CLOSE - 20 seconds]
Synthesise the episode's theme in one or two sentences.
End with a forward-looking question or signal to watch.
Use one of Brett's closing patterns.
```

### Template A guidelines
- Total word count target: 750-900 words.
- Pacing: the hook should feel fast; stories should breathe; Brett's takes are punchy.
- If one story is significantly stronger, give it Story 1 position and slightly more time.
- The cross-domain connection in the second take is the episode's payoff. Don't force it if it isn't natural, but always attempt it.

## Template B: Deep Dive (10 minutes)

Used when a single story is big enough to warrant extended treatment. Typically for major geopolitical shifts, breakthrough scientific findings, or significant policy decisions.

```
[HOOK - 30 seconds]
Set up the big question this episode will answer.
Make the audience feel the stakes immediately.
Example: "The chip war just entered a new phase, and most people missed the
moment it happened."

[CONTEXT - 2 minutes]
The background most people don't know or have forgotten.
- Historical context: what led to this moment.
- Key players and their motivations.
- The conventional wisdom Brett is about to challenge or complicate.

[THE STORY - 3 minutes]
What happened and why it matters.
- The specific event, decision, or finding.
- Primary source attribution.
- The details that change the interpretation.
- What the mainstream coverage got right and what it oversimplified.

[BRETT'S ANALYSIS - 2 minutes]
Brett's extended take.
- Connections to other domains (geopolitics ↔ technology ↔ economics).
- Second-order and third-order effects.
- Who wins and who loses from this development.
- Historical parallels (used carefully, not lazily).

[WHAT TO WATCH - 1 minute]
Concrete signals and indicators the audience should track.
- Specific metrics, dates, decisions, or actors to monitor.
- "If X happens within Y timeframe, it means Z."

[CLOSE - 30 seconds]
The question nobody is asking yet.
One sentence that reframes the entire story.
Use one of Brett's closing patterns.
```

### Template B guidelines
- Total word count target: 1,500-1,800 words.
- This template requires at least 3 high-quality source articles on the same topic.
- The Context section is crucial: it's what separates Brett from a news reader.
- Brett's Analysis should feel like the intellectual centre of gravity.
- Use this template no more than twice per week to keep it special.

## Template C: Weekly Roundup (15 minutes)

Published once per week (typically Friday or Saturday). Reviews the week's biggest stories and connects them into a coherent narrative.

```
[HOOK - 30 seconds]
Name the theme of the week in one sentence.
Example: "This was the week the old rules stopped working — in three very
different ways."

[STORY 1 - 3 minutes]
The biggest story of the week.
- Full narrative with context.
- Why Brett chose this as the #1 story.
- What happened during the week that changed its significance.

[STORY 2 - 3 minutes]
The most surprising story.
- A development that defied expectations or conventional wisdom.
- Why the surprise matters more than the headline.

[STORY 3 - 3 minutes]
The most underreported story.
- Something important that didn't get enough attention.
- Why Brett thinks the audience should know about it.
- Often comes from Pillar 3 (Quirky/Outlier) or a niche source.

[BRETT'S SYNTHESIS - 3 minutes]
Connect all three stories into a single argument or observation.
- The pattern or trend they reveal together.
- What this week tells us about the direction of the next month/quarter/year.
- Brett's strongest opinion of the week goes here.

[PREVIEW - 1 minute]
What's coming next week.
- Scheduled events (summits, earnings, court decisions, launches).
- Stories Brett is tracking that haven't broken yet.
- A question he'll be trying to answer.

[CLOSE - 30 seconds]
Weekly sign-off.
Slightly warmer tone than daily episodes.
Acknowledge the audience directly.
Use one of Brett's closing patterns.
```

### Template C guidelines
- Total word count target: 2,200-2,700 words.
- The three stories should span at least two of Brett's three pillars.
- The Synthesis section is the centrepiece. It should feel like an original argument, not a summary.
- The Preview section helps build habit: listeners come back Monday because they know what's coming.
- Tone can be slightly more reflective than daily episodes.

---

## Section-Level Rules (All Templates)

1. **Attribution is mandatory.** Every factual claim must reference its source by name. "According to Reuters..." or "The FT reported on Tuesday..."
2. **Transitions must feel natural.** No "Moving on to our next story..." — use Brett's conversational style. "Now, completely different world, but stay with me here..."
3. **Numbers need context.** Don't say "$4 billion" without helping the audience understand scale. "That's roughly what Australia spends on defence in a month."
4. **Quotes should be rare and impactful.** One or two per episode maximum. They should be memorable, not just informational.
5. **Every section must earn its time.** If a section can't justify its allocated duration with substance, cut it shorter rather than padding.
